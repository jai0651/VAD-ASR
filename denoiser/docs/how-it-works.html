Stage 00 · The goal

Speech buried in noise

Your microphone captures one stream of numbers — air pressure sampled 48,000 times a second. That single stream is really two things added together: the speech you want, and the noise you don't (a fan, keyboard, traffic, café babble). Written simply:

mic(t)  =  speech(t)  +  noise(t)

The denoiser's whole job is to estimate speech(t) and throw away the rest. The trouble is that in the raw waveform the two are hopelessly tangled — every sample is a sum, and you can't un-add two numbers from their total. Look at the waveform below: the noisy and clean signals are almost indistinguishable to the eye.

Stage 01 · Representation

Into the frequency domain

Speech and noise look tangled in time, but they separate beautifully in frequency. Voiced speech is built from a fundamental pitch and a stack of evenly-spaced harmonics — sharp horizontal ridges of energy. Most noise is broadband — a diffuse haze spread across all frequencies. Once you can see that difference, you can act on it.

To get there we use the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT). Instead of transforming the whole signal at once, we slide a short window along it and take a Fourier transform of each little slice:

  • Cut the audio into overlapping frames — here a 960-sample window (20 ms) that hops forward 480 samples (10 ms) each step, so frames overlap 50%.
  • Run an FFT on each frame → 481 frequency bins (0 Hz up to 24 kHz, spaced 50 Hz apart).
  • Each bin is a complex number: a magnitude (how much of that frequency) and a phase (its timing). Stack the magnitudes of every frame side by side and you get a spectrogram — a picture of sound.

Here is a real spectrogram, computed live in your browser from a synthetic voice + noise. Toggle between the noisy input and the clean target. Notice how the harmonic ridges of speech stay put while the noise is the speckled floor between and around them — that is the structure the model exploits.

Stage 02 · The classic approach

The classic fix — and why it's not enough

If speech is the bright ridges and noise is the floor, the obvious move is to build a mask: for every time-frequency bin, decide how much to keep. Multiply each bin's magnitude by a gain between 0 (silence this bin) and 1 (keep it). Bins dominated by speech → gain near 1; bins that are mostly noise → gain near 0. This is the idea behind spectral subtraction, Wiener filtering, and the classic RNNoise.

Ŝ(t,f)  =  g(t,f) · X(t,f)    , 0 ≤ g ≤ 1

It works, up to a point. But a single real-valued gain per bin has two hard limits:

  • It can't fix phase. A gain only scales magnitude. But when noise corrupts a bin it also corrupts the timing (phase) of that frequency. Keep the noisy phase and you cap how clean the result can sound.
  • It can't rebuild structure. When speech and noise share a bin, the gain must compromise — turn it down and you dull the voice, turn it up and you keep noise. Aggressive masking produces musical noise: warbly, watery artifacts. It has no way to reconstruct what the speech should have been in that bin.

The key limitation

A per-bin mask treats every bin in isolation. But speech is highly correlated across neighbouring time frames — a harmonic ridge is smooth and predictable from one 10 ms frame to the next. A mask throws that information away. DeepFilterNet's headline idea is to use it.

Stage 03 · DeepFilterNet, idea one

ERB bands — predict 32 numbers, not 481

Predicting a separate gain for all 481 frequency bins every 10 ms is expensive and wasteful — your ear doesn't resolve high frequencies that finely anyway. Human hearing groups frequencies into critical bands that get wider as pitch rises. DeepFilterNet mirrors this with the ERB scale (Equivalent Rectangular Bandwidth): it collapses the 481 bins into just 32 ERB bands — narrow and detailed in the low frequencies where the voice lives, broad and coarse up high.

This ERB gain stage is Stage 1 of the network. It captures the coarse spectral envelope — the overall shape of "where is the voice right now" — and does the bulk of the noise suppression across the entire 0–24 kHz range, cheaply. It behaves like a smart, perceptually-weighted version of the classic mask.

Stage 04 · DeepFilterNet, idea two

Deep filtering — a tiny filter across time

The ERB gains clean up the broad picture, but they're too coarse for the perceptually critical low band (roughly 0–5 kHz, the first 96 bins) where the voice's harmonics and intelligibility live. For that band DeepFilterNet does something cleverer than a gain. This is the operation the whole method is named after: deep filtering.

Instead of multiplying a bin by one number, it applies a small complex filter of order 5 — five learned complex coefficients that combine the bin with its neighbours across recent time frames:

Ŝ(t,f)  =  Σi=0…4  ci(t,f) · X(t−i+ℓ, f)

Why this is powerful:

  • It corrects phase — complex coefficients rotate as well as scale, so the reconstructed speech isn't stuck with the noisy timing.
  • It reconstructs, not just attenuates. By blending several frames it can fill in a harmonic that noise partially masked, using the predictable structure of the surrounding frames — like interpolating a briefly-obscured line from its trajectory.
  • It suppresses noise between harmonics far more precisely than a coarse gain, which is what kills the "musical noise" artifact.

Two stages, two jobs

ERB gains = coarse, full-band envelope cleanup (cheap, perceptual). Deep filtering = fine, phase-aware reconstruction of the low speech band (precise). The network runs both and combines them.

Stage 06 · Time and latency

Memory, lookahead, and the latency budget

Two design choices make this a temporal model rather than a frame-by-frame one, and both matter enormously for realtime use.

The GRU gives it memory

A GRU (gated recurrent unit) is a small recurrent network that maintains a hidden state summarising everything it has seen so far. This is how the model knows the difference between a steady fan (present for seconds — clearly noise) and a spoken vowel (structured, evolving — clearly speech). Denoising the current 10 ms frame well requires knowing the recent past. Hold this thought — it's the entire reason realtime streaming is tricky (next section).

Lookahead costs latency

The deep filter also peeks 2 frames into the future (the +ℓ lookahead term from Stage 04). Seeing a little ahead makes reconstruction more accurate, but you can't process a frame until those future frames have arrived — so it imposes an unavoidable algorithmic delay of 2 frames = 20 ms. Stage 07 · The realtime trick

Making it realtime: warm context

Here's the catch that the GRU's memory creates. DeepFilterNet3's model, as shipped, does not carry its recurrent state between separate calls — every time you hand it a chunk of audio, its memory starts from zero. For offline files that's fine: you pass the whole recording in one call and the GRU runs across all of it.

But a live call must be processed in tiny chunks as audio trickles in. If you denoise each small chunk independently, the GRU is cold every time — it never accumulates the context it needs. I measured exactly how bad this is on your machine. Small blocks don't just underperform; at 20–30 ms they make the audio worse than doing nothing:

The fix — the strategy your DeepFilterNetBackend actually uses — is warm left-context. For every small output block, we don't feed the model just that block. We prepend a few hundred milliseconds of the recent past audio and run the model over the whole window, but keep only the newest block's output. The GRU warms up over the context every single time, so the block we emit is denoised with full memory — yet we only ever output fresh audio.

And it works. Re-running with warm context at a 40 ms hop recovers essentially all the quality of whole-file processing — the sweet spot your backend ships with is 40 ms hop + 200 ms context:

The pipeline, end to end

mic → capture → warm-context denoise → virtual audio device → your call. Two audio streams (mic in, virtual device out) are decoupled by a worker thread and a ring buffer so the neural inference never blocks the audio callbacks. The call app just sees "BlackHole" as its microphone and hears a clean voice.
