3.1The problem, before any solution

Here is the situation, and it is worth sitting with for a moment before reading on.

Someone says "cat". You compute features and get 400 frames. Your transcript is 3 characters. Your training data contains exactly these two things — the audio and the string — and nothing else. Nobody has told you that frames 51–63 are the c.

frames: 1 2 3 4 5 6 ... 398 399 400 ? ? ? ? ? ? ? ? ? target: c a t ↑ which frames? nobody knows, and nobody will tell you

So: how do you compute a loss? A neural network needs to be told what the right answer is at each output position. You have 400 output positions and 3 labels.

Two obvious ideas, and why both fail

"Just spread them evenly." Assign frames 1–133 to c, 134–266 to a, 267–400 to t. This is wrong and confidently so: the t in "cat" is a 20 ms burst, the a might run 200 ms. You would be training the model to call silence "a". Worse, the error is different for every utterance, so it never averages out.

"Label the data properly, then." This is what speech recognition actually did for thirty years — a forced aligner, built from a pronunciation dictionary and an HMM, produced frame labels and you trained on those. It works, and it means you need a lexicon for every word, a phoneme set, and a separate alignment model that itself has to be trained. That is the entire pre-2015 pipeline you are trying not to build.

The question the whole field is organised around: can you train on the (audio, text) pair directly — never producing an alignment at all?

The idea: don't choose an alignment. Use all of them.

Here is the move. Instead of picking one alignment and hoping it is right, let the model emit one symbol per frame, and define a rule that squashes any such 400-symbol string down to text. Then say: the probability of "cat" is the probability of every 400-symbol string that squashes to "cat", added up.

The model is never told where the c is. It is only told that whatever it emitted had better collapse to "cat". It discovers the alignment itself, as a side effect of maximising that sum.

That is CTC, and everything else is detail.

3.2CTC, built up one piece at a time

Piece 1 — the collapse rule, and the problem it creates

Start with the obvious rule: merge adjacent repeats. The model emits 400 symbols; identical neighbours are the same sound held longer.

c c c a a a a a t t   →   c a t     ✓

Now try it on a word with a genuine double letter. Say "hello". The model wants to emit two ls. But adjacent identical symbols get merged — so there is no 400-symbol string that collapses to "hello". Every attempt gives "helo":

h h e e l l l l o o   →   h e l o    ✗  the double l is gone

Piece 2 — the blank, which exists to solve exactly that

Add one extra symbol to the vocabulary: blank, written –. Two rules now, in order: merge repeats, then delete blanks.

h h e – l l – l o o   →   h e – l – l o   →   h e l l o    ✓

The blank between the two ls survives the merge step and breaks them apart; it is then deleted. That is the blank's first job, and it is the one people forget: a blank is not silence, it is a separator.

Its second job is more obvious once you see it. The model has 400 frames and only 3 things to say. Most frames it has nothing new to contribute — it is mid-vowel, or in a pause, or unsure. The blank lets it say "nothing new this frame" without being wrong.

Check your understanding Which of these collapse to "too"? t t – o o o · t – o – o · t t o o o
Answers: the first gives "to" (the two o's merge — wrong), the second gives "too" ✓, the third gives "to" (wrong). Only an explicit blank between the o's produces the double.

Piece 3 — the loss: adding up a number you cannot enumerate

Now the sum. Many 400-symbol strings collapse to "cat", and the loss is the negative log of their total probability:

L = −log [ P(c c c a a a a a t t) + P(– c – a a a t t –) + P(c – – a a t t t t) + … ]

How many terms is that? For 400 frames over a 29-symbol vocabulary, the number of valid alignments is astronomically large — you could not write them down, let alone add them up.

The escape is that you don't have to. Alignments share prefixes. Every path that has emitted "c a" by frame 200 is, from frame 201 onward, facing exactly the same remaining problem — it does not matter how it got there. So you never track individual paths; you track how much total probability has reached each (frame, position-in-target) cell, and sweep forward one frame at a time.

frame → 1 2 3 4 5 ... target – α ─── α ─── α ─── α ─── α every arrow: stay, or advance c ╲ │╲ │╲ │╲ │ the value in each cell is the TOTAL – ╲ │ ╲ │ ╲ │ ╲ │ probability of all paths reaching it a ╲ │ ╲ │ ╲ │ ╲ │ – ↓ ↓ ↓ ↓ 400 columns, ~7 rows — trivial to compute t – answer = last two cells of the final column

That is the forward algorithm, and it turns an astronomically large sum into a grid you sweep once. It is the same trick as any dynamic program: exponentially many paths, polynomially many states those paths can be in.

In our code this is one line — torch.nn.CTCLoss — and the reason it is worth understanding anyway is that its edge cases bite. If the target is longer than the number of frames there is no valid path at all, and PyTorch silently returns a zero gradient rather than an error (§12.4).

Piece 4 — decoding, where the same sum reappears

At inference you want the most likely text, but the model gives you per-frame symbol probabilities. The cheap answer, greedy decoding, takes the argmax at each frame and collapses it. That commits to one alignment — and the most likely alignment is not necessarily the most likely text, because the text's probability is spread across many alignments.

Prefix beam search fixes this by scoring text prefixes rather than paths, keeping the top-k. Its one subtlety is the one you would predict from Piece 2: each prefix must track two probabilities — the probability it ends in a blank, and the probability it ends in a real symbol. Without that split you cannot tell "the model repeated the o" from "the model wrote a second o", which is the "too" versus "to" question again.

Where this leaves us. We can now train on (audio, text) pairs with no aligner, no lexicon, and no phoneme set. That is a genuinely large win. What we have not got is any notion of what English looks like.

The flaw that defines everything after CTC

Look again at how the loss factorises: the model emits each frame's symbol independently, given the audio. Frame 200 does not know what frame 199 emitted. So nothing in the model knows that a q is followed by a u, or that "shold" is not a word.

This is not a bug to be fixed with more data — it is structural, and it is exactly what our Module 2 output looks like:

hyp: he inl san er mianthes tlen kis roint the stieia sord meai wen
ref: but in less than five minutes the staircase groaned beneath an extraordinary weight

Notice the kind of error. The rhythm is right, many vowels are right — it is not acoustically stupid. It is orthographically impossible. No English word looks like "mianthes".

There are only two ways out, and they are the next two architectures: bolt a language model onto the beam search from outside, or put the language model inside the network by letting it condition on what it has already emitted.

3.3Whisper: what 680k hours + a decoder buys

log-mel (3000×80, 30 s window) ▼ Transformer encoder (self-attention over audio) "listen" ▼ cross-attention Transformer decoder ─────────────────────▶ tokens "write, while glancing back" <|en|> <|transcribe|> The staircase groaned ... <|eot|>
  • Alignment is implicit in cross-attention: each generated token looks back at the audio frames it needs. No blanks, no collapse rule.
  • The decoder IS a language model — trained jointly, it fixes CTC's independence problem, which is why Whisper's output has correct spelling and punctuation. The dark side: when audio is empty/garbled, a language model with a microphone keeps talking — Whisper's infamous hallucinated sentences on silence. Two of our pipeline choices exist purely to manage this: the endpointer never sends sub-250 ms blips, and condition_on_previous_text=False stops hallucination loops from compounding across turns.
  • Trained on ~680k hours of weakly-labeled web audio. Your model saw ~25 minutes. That five-orders-of-magnitude gap, not the architecture, is most of the quality difference (the Transformer helps, but a Transformer on 25 minutes would fail too).

3.4Streaming ASR vs. what we built

Our pipeline transcribes whole utterances after the endpointer closes a turn. Honest trade-off: simplest, most accurate per utterance (full context), but no live captions, and the ASR cost lands entirely inside the turnaround budget.

True streaming (word-by-word partials while you talk) needs a model whose encoder is causal — RNN-T, or chunked-attention Conformers. That's what powers live captions and lets voice agents do smarter things: semantic endpointing (read the partial transcript to decide if the user is done) and speculative LLM prefill on partial text. The production pattern at Deepgram/AssemblyAI/Google is RNN-T-style streaming with partial results that stabilize over time (early words may be revised).

Why we chose utterance-based anyway Most open self-hosted voice agents (pipecat, LiveKit examples) do exactly this with faster-whisper: at 0.08 RTF the whole turn transcribes in ~300 ms, which fits inside the 700 ms endpoint wait that exists regardless. Streaming partials matter when turns are long, captions are the product, or you're doing semantic endpointing.

3.5Inference engines: why "faster-whisper" isn't just whisper

Training frameworks (PyTorch) optimize for flexibility and gradients. Inference engines optimize for one thing: forward pass throughput. faster-whisper = Whisper's exact weights executed by CTranslate2:

  • Int8 weight quantization — weights stored as 8-bit ints (4× smaller, CPU SIMD-friendly), activations computed in higher precision. ~4× faster on CPU, WER essentially unchanged. Same idea as GGUF/llama.cpp quantization for LLMs.
  • Fused kernels — attention+layernorm+GEMM fused into single ops; no Python in the loop, no autograd bookkeeping.
  • Batching machinery — multiple streams share one forward pass (the GPU economics of serving; see the production page).

The general lesson: train in one tool, serve in another — PyTorch → ONNX Runtime (how Silero and Kokoro ship), CTranslate2, TensorRT, vLLM. The export seam is a permanent fixture of production ML.

3.6Evaluation, properly

  • WER (word error rate) = word-level edit distance ÷ reference words — the industry headline number. CER (ours) is the character version, kinder to small models and language-agnostic.
  • Always report seen vs held-out separately — your Module 2b's ~0 train CER vs high held-out CER is the data-hunger gap made visible, and it's why "we hit 2% WER" means nothing without knowing the test set.
  • Real evals slice by domain: accents, noise (SNR sweeps), telephony 8 kHz, far-field, code-switching. LibriSpeech-clean is the easiest public benchmark — treat numbers on it as upper bounds.

3.7If you want to push the scratch model

  1. Data first: train-clean-100 (100 h) instead of 5.4 h of dev-clean; that alone moves held-out CER more than any architecture change.
  2. SpecAugment: random time/frequency masks on the mel — the single highest-value regularizer in speech, ~5 lines.
  3. Subword units (BPE) instead of characters: shorter targets, easier CTC.
  4. Conformer blocks instead of BiGRU: convolution + self-attention, the modern encoder default.
  5. Shallow LM fusion: add a char-LM score inside your beam search — directly attacks CTC's independence assumption.
All of that is now built Module 6 does exactly these things: a Conformer encoder with relative-position attention, BPE subwords, and an autoregressive decoder trained jointly with CTC — which is the principled version of "shallow LM fusion", since the language model is learned on the same encoder rather than bolted on at decode time. It also streams, which the BiGRU on this page structurally cannot. Read this page first, then that one: the point of keeping both models in the repo is that you can run them on the same audio and hear the difference.
