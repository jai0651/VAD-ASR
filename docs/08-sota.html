8.2Denoise: why 24K parameters beat 20 years of statistics

Module 5 measured the classical algorithm as competitive on stationary noise. The architectural leap is about everything else.

What the learned models add

  • Recognition instead of stationarity. MCRA assumes noise is stable enough that its running minimum estimates the floor. True for fans, hiss and hum; completely false for a door slam, a keyboard, a dog, another person talking — the noises that actually ruin calls. A trained model has seen thousands of hours of those and identifies them by their time-frequency shape.
  • Complex masks. GTCRN estimates a complex-valued mask, so it corrects phase. No gain-only method can, at any parameter count. This is the hard ceiling on spectral.py.
  • Capacity in the right places. GTCRN gets Krisp-class quality from 23.7K parameters and 39.6 MMACs/s using grouped convolutions, sub-band feature extraction and a dual-path RNN across the frequency axis. Enhancement is a per-bin regression with strong local structure; it needs the right inductive bias, not scale. For comparison, ULCNet matches DeepFilterNet2 subjectively at ~25% of its complexity, and Fast-ULCNet halves that again by swapping GRUs for FastGRNNs.
  • Streaming as an architectural property. GTCRN and DTLN carry explicit caches frame-to-frame, so each hop is exactly one forward pass. DeepFilterNet3 doesn't, which is why real-time DFN is a Rust library and why the denoiser/ sub-project has to re-process 200 ms of context every hop to use it — 23% of a core versus 3%.

What to actually ship

16 kHz voice agents: GTCRN. 48 kHz consumer audio: DeepFilterNet3. Ultra-constrained embedded: ULCNet-class. And measure the ASR effect before you enable it globally — §7.5.

8.3VAD and the thing that replaced it

Frame VAD is a solved, tiny problem

Both leaders are small models over log-mel-ish features with recurrent state. Silero v5 is ~2 MB, 32 ms windows, and its moat is data — ~100 languages and thousands of hours of hard negatives (music, keyboard, babble). TEN VAD is the 2026 challenger: better precision at lower compute and memory, and — the part that matters for agents — much faster speech→non-speech transition detection, where Silero is known to lag by a few hundred milliseconds. In a turn-taking budget, several hundred ms of VAD lag is not a rounding error; it is most of your responsiveness budget.

Our result, restated Module 1b changed nothing but the data and turned a VAD that pinned P≈1 on room ambience into one that reads 0.01–0.04 on noise and 0.78 on speech. The architecture in src/vad/model.py — three conv layers over log-mel — is genuinely the right shape. The remaining soft spots (babble 0.50, quiet speech 0.47) are also data problems, and Module 5 just moved babble to 0.39 with no retraining at all.

The real 2026 state of the art is not VAD at all

A fixed silence timeout — what src/pipeline/endpointing.py implements — forces an unwinnable trade: 500 ms cuts people off mid-thought, 900 ms feels laggy. The fix is not a better threshold; it's a different question. Semantic / audio-native turn detection asks "does this person expect me to reply now?" from prosody and syntax — falling pitch and a completed clause mean go, a held vowel or "my number is four two…" means wait.

  • Smart Turn v3.x (Pipecat/Daily) — a Whisper-tiny encoder plus a linear classifier, ~8M params, 8 MB int8 ONNX, ~12 ms on a modern CPU. BSD-2 with open weights, open training code and open datasets — which is exactly why it's the right thing to build against. v3.1 added real human audio over synthetic; v3.2 added short-utterance handling and café/office noise.
  • LiveKit turn-detector — the transcript-conditioned school: run the text through a small LM and ask whether the utterance is complete. Cheaper to train, but it inherits ASR latency and loses all prosody.

This repo has neither. That's the honest gap in the pipeline — and it is also the most attackable one, which is the whole subject of §9.

8.4ASR: four architectural eras, and where ours sits

1. HMM-GMM + pronunciation lexicon pre-2014, hand-built pipeline 2. CTC encoder → per-frame labels DeepSpeech · ← this repo 3. RNN-T / TDT encoder + prediction net Parakeet · streaming-native 4. attention enc-dec / speech-LLM Whisper · Qwen3-ASR · Voxtral

Why CTC was superseded

CTC's defining assumption is conditional independence: given the audio, each output frame's label is predicted independently of the others. That is what makes the dynamic-programming loss tractable, and it is why our decoder needs a beam search (and really wants a language model) to produce spellable English. The model has no internal notion of "having just emitted a Q, a U is likely". RNN-Transducer fixes exactly this by adding a prediction network over the output history — a built-in LM — while keeping strict monotonic, streaming-friendly alignment. TDT (Token-and-Duration Transducer) then lets the model emit a token and a duration in one step, so it skips frames instead of emitting blanks; that's the trick behind Parakeet's extraordinary throughput.

The 2026 options, honestly compared

What would actually have to change here

This is the stage where "just add data" is wrong. In rough order of impact:

  1. The encoder. BiGRU → Conformer (conv for local phonetics + self-attention for long context). Roughly 12 layers, d_model 256, 4 heads, conv kernel 31, subsampling ×4. This alone is most of the modern-ASR quality jump and is ~15M params — trainable on an M4.
  2. Causality. Bidirectional means the model waits for the whole utterance. Chunked/limited-context attention or a unidirectional encoder is mandatory for a real agent.
  3. The objective. CTC → hybrid CTC+attention (train both heads, use CTC for alignment and fast decoding) or RNN-T if you want true streaming.
  4. Tokens. Characters → a 256–1024-entry SentencePiece vocabulary. Fewer decoder steps, better implicit spelling.
  5. Augmentation. SpecAugment is not optional; it is worth more than doubling the data on small corpora.
  6. Data. Then data. dev-clean subset → train-clean-100 → train-960 → the open corpora in §8.8.

8.5TTS: three eras, and why Griffin-Lim is the wall

1. autoregressive + attention Tacotron 2 · ← this repo attention CAN break 2. non-autoregressive + duration FastSpeech 2 · robust, fast, flat prosody 3. generative: diffusion / flow-matching / codec-LM natural prosody, zero-shot cloning

Our Tacotron-mini is era 1 and hits every one of era 1's known failures: exposure bias, attention stalls, and a stop head that has to be guarded with heuristics. Era 2 removed the failure mode entirely by predicting a duration per token and expanding — no attention to break. Era 3 treats speech as a generative problem: flow-matching over mel (F5-TTS style) or an LM over neural-codec tokens (Orpheus, Chatterbox), which is what unlocked 5-second zero-shot voice cloning and genuinely expressive prosody.

The ceiling that data cannot lift Our vocoder is Griffin-Lim: it iteratively guesses the phase that was thrown away when we took magnitudes. It is a beautiful thing to implement and it is a hard quality ceiling. A neural vocoder (HiFi-GAN, Vocos) or a codec decoder is a learned mel→waveform map and is the single biggest perceptual upgrade available to Module 4 — bigger than any amount of extra training data for the acoustic model.

8.6Cascade vs. end-to-end speech-to-speech

The pipeline in this repo — denoise → VAD → endpoint → ASR → LLM → TTS — is a cascade, and cascades are still what almost every production voice agent ships. Their weakness is structural: they are turn-based, they throw away everything non-lexical (tone, hesitation, emotion) at the ASR boundary, and their latency is a sum.

Full-duplex speech-to-speech models (Moshi, and the Thinker-Talker family like Qwen3-Omni) model the user's speech and the system's speech as parallel streams, so overlap, backchannels and interruptions are native rather than bolted on. Reported response latencies sit around 200–260 ms versus the ~800 ms+ a good cascade achieves.

Why cascades still win in production Control. You can swap the LLM, enforce a system prompt, log a transcript, run compliance checks, hot-fix a pronunciation, and bill per stage. End-to-end models give up most of that. The pragmatic 2026 architecture is a cascade with the turn-taking layer upgraded to an audio-native model — which is precisely the gap §9 targets.

Five distinct gaps. Naming them separately is what makes the plan tractable.

Two measured facts from this repo make the point concretely. Recipe beats everything for cheapness: in both Module 1b and Module 4, selecting the checkpoint on the metric we cared about rather than taking the last one changed the shipped model materially — the VAD's best hard-case score was 0.806 at step 2750 while the final step scored 0.699, and the TTS judge oscillated 0.96 → 0.61 with three regressions along the way. Architecture beats data where there's a ceiling: no quantity of LibriSpeech makes Griffin-Lim estimate phase.

Recipe A — a real ASR you could actually ship, on an M4

Target: WER in the low teens on LibriSpeech test-clean. Not Parakeet, but a genuine recogniser rather than a demo.

  • Model: Conformer encoder, 12 layers, d_model 256, 4 heads, FF 1024, conv kernel 31, ×4 subsampling → ~15M params.
  • Objective: hybrid CTC + attention decoder (λ ≈ 0.3 CTC). Train both; decode with CTC prefix beam + attention rescoring.
  • Tokens: SentencePiece unigram, vocab 256–512.
  • Data: train-clean-100 first (fast iteration), then train-960.
  • Augmentation: SpecAugment (2 freq masks ≤27 bins, 2 time masks ≤ 5% of frames), speed perturb 0.9/1.0/1.1, MUSAN noise at 5–20 dB SNR, RIR reverb on ~30%. On 100 h this is worth more than another 100 h.
  • Optimiser: AdamW, Noam/warmup-cosine, 10k warmup steps, peak LR 2e-3, weight decay 1e-2, grad clip 5.0, dynamic batching by total frames.
  • Precision: bf16 where MPS supports it; gradient accumulation to reach an effective batch of ~2–4 minutes of audio per step.
  • Selection: average the last ~10 checkpoints (a standard, nearly-free ~0.3 WER win) and select on held-out WER, never on loss.
  • Budget: days, not hours, on an M4 for train-960. Rent an A100 for a day if the goal is the number rather than the learning.

Recipe B — a TTS that clears the Griffin-Lim ceiling

  • Step 1, biggest win: keep Tacotron-mini, replace Griffin-Lim with a pretrained neural vocoder (HiFi-GAN or Vocos) on the same 80-mel configuration. Days of work, an enormous perceptual jump, no retraining of the acoustic model.
  • Step 2: replace the attention decoder with a duration predictor (FastSpeech-style). Extract durations from your existing model's attention or from a forced aligner (MFA). Removes exposure bias and attention collapse permanently, and makes inference non-autoregressive → far lower TTFB.
  • Step 3: LJSpeech (24 h, single speaker) → VCTK (multi-speaker) → an Emilia subset for spontaneous prosody.
  • Selection: keep the ASR-judge you already built — synthesise, transcribe with Whisper, select on CER. It caught three regressions in Module 4 that loss curves did not.

Recipe C — a denoiser (train GTCRN-class yourself)

  • Data: DNS Challenge synthesis — clean speech × noise × RIR mixed on the fly at −5…20 dB SNR. Generate mixtures per epoch rather than pre-baking; the augmentation space is the dataset.
  • Target: complex ratio mask or complex spectral mapping, not a magnitude mask. That's the whole point.
  • Loss: compressed-magnitude + complex MSE (power-law compression ≈0.3), optionally plus an SI-SDR term. Perceptual losses matter more here than in any other stage.
  • Eval: PESQ-WB, STOI, SI-SDR on VoiceBank-DEMAND — already implemented in denoiser/evaluate.py.
  • Feasible on an M4? Yes. 23.7K parameters. This is the most achievable "train a SOTA-class model yourself" target in the whole stack.

8.9The AI-engineering layer that closes the last mile

Model quality is roughly half of "best in class". The rest is this:

  • Build the eval set before the model. Not a benchmark — a set of the specific things that break. Module 1b's "hard cases" (zeros, hum, babble, quiet speech) took ten minutes to write and are the reason a shipped checkpoint was chosen correctly. A metric that reflects your product failure modes is worth more than a leaderboard.
  • Select checkpoints on that metric. "Last" is not "best". Both training runs in this repo showed edge-case behaviour oscillating while the loss curve sat flat.
  • Measure p50 and p95. Latency is long-tailed and users feel the tail. src/pipeline/metrics.py already decomposes the turnaround into endpoint wait + ASR + respond + TTS-first-chunk — that decomposition is what tells you which stage to fix.
  • Quantise and export. int8 ONNX / CTranslate2 is typically 4× smaller and 2–3× faster for <1% relative WER. Measured here: whisper base.en int8 runs at RTF 0.08 with CER 0.012 on dev-clean. Smart Turn ships 8 MB int8 and runs in 12 ms.
  • Distil. Whisper large-v3-turbo is literally "keep the encoder, cut the decoder from 32 layers to 4" — a reminder that in encoder-decoder ASR the decoder is where the latency hides and where the compression is cheapest.
  • Streaming beats accuracy for perceived quality. First-token latency dominates user perception. A slightly worse model that starts speaking in 300 ms beats a better one that starts in 900 ms.
  • Log the audio you get wrong. The compounding advantage in voice AI is a labelled corpus of your own failures. Every open dataset is available to your competitors; your call recordings are not.

8.10Where a voice-AI company should actually invest

Ranked by defensibility per unit of effort, given that Parakeet, Whisper, Kokoro and GTCRN are all free:

  1. Turn-taking on your channel. Everyone's models are trained on clean wideband mic audio. Telephony is 8 kHz μ-law, often transcoded twice. This is a real, narrow, measurable gap — see §9.
  2. Domain adaptation of an open ASR. Fine-tuning Parakeet or Whisper on your vertical's vocabulary (names, SKUs, addresses, code-switching) beats any general model on your traffic, for a fraction of pretraining cost.
  3. The orchestration layer. Barge-in, endpointing policy, interruption recovery, backchannels, latency budgeting. Almost no papers, huge product impact — and it's the layer this repo builds fully.
  4. Evaluation infrastructure. A replayable corpus of real calls with turn-level labels, so any model change is measurable in hours.
  5. Pretraining a general ASR/TTS from scratch. Last. It is the most expensive way to reach parity with a free model.
