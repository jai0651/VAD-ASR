The Crux

The synthesis chapter. What this project actually taught, the mistakes that taught it, the mental models worth keeping, and how to explain any of it to someone else. Read this first, or read it last — it works both ways.

← contents · the crux

0.1The project in three sentences

A complete real-time voice agent — noise suppression, voice activity detection, turn detection, speech recognition, a language model, and speech synthesis — with every model built from scratch and a production-grade alternate behind the same interface, so the two can be run on the same audio and compared.

The scratch models exist to be understood and then replaced; the pipeline around them — orchestration, barge-in, endpointing, latency budgeting — is the part that is genuinely production-shaped and genuinely ours.

The most valuable output is not any model. It is a catalogue of specific, measured failures and what each one taught.

0.5The failure catalogue — the actual education

Everything below was measured, not read. Grouped by the lesson, because the lesson repeats across very different-looking bugs.

Theme 1 — the metric was lying to you

Failure · the vocoder metric was rigged in the baseline's favour We scored a neural vocoder against Griffin-Lim on multi-resolution STFT distance. Griffin-Lim lost on phase and won the comparison — because MR-STFT is a magnitude metric, and magnitude consistency is literally Griffin-Lim's optimisation objective.
Lesson: check whether your metric measures the thing your baseline optimises. Adding SI-SDR (time-domain, phase-sensitive) reversed the result: +5.4 dB for the neural model.
Failure · CTC loss "should" start near ln(V) — it shouldn't Our own validation gate flagged a healthy model as broken. With reduction="mean" PyTorch divides by target length while the path probability accumulates over frames, so an untrained CTC loss is ≈ (frames/token) × ln V ≈ 41, not ln V ≈ 6.9.
Lesson: know what your loss is normalised by. A validator that cries wolf is worse than no validator.
Failure · held-out set shares 39/39 speakers with training Every WER we quoted was speaker-dependent, therefore optimistic, and not comparable to any published number.
Lesson: "held out" means held out along the axis you care about. For ASR that is speakers, not utterances.
Failure · checkpoint averaging made the model worse Averaging the last 5 checkpoints gave WER 0.659 against a best single of 0.612. The technique assumes the checkpoints orbit one basin; ours spanned the whole run (WER 0.86 → 0.61) and were in different basins.
Lesson: every technique has a precondition. Now we average the best-K by held-out WER, and keep it only if it wins.

Theme 2 — it failed silently

Failure · CTC blank collapse The model learned that emitting blank on every frame is a safe, finite-loss answer. 100% of frames blank, entropy 0.82 of a possible 6.93, greedy decode = empty string. Invisible in the loss (flat at a plausible value) and invisible in WER (pinned at 1.0, indistinguishable from "early").
Lesson: log a signal that is specific to the failure mode — blank-frame fraction — not just the loss and the headline metric.
Failure · torch.cuda.is_available() returned True while a conv failed Twice. Once with a CUDA-13 wheel on a CUDA-12 driver, once with cuDNN 9.20 against a 9.10 driver. Both would have trained silently on CPU or crashed hours in.
Lesson: verify a capability by exercising it, not by asking whether it exists. The bootstrap now runs a real convolution.
Failure · tokenizer trained on the wrong corpus, then regenerated under a checkpoint A tokenizer fitted on dev-clean (2,557 utts) was silently reused for a train-clean-100 run (28,500) because the only check was vocabulary size. Later a validation run regenerated the file in place, remapping every token id under an existing checkpoint — which then emitted real structure with corrupted spelling.
Lesson: artefacts that must agree should carry a fingerprint of each other, not just a filename.
Failure · DTLN at the wrong hop destroyed reconstruction Reusing a 512/256 overlap-add shell for a model trained at 512/128 looked harmless. 19 dB SNR in, 6 dB out.
Lesson: framing parameters are part of the model, not tuning knobs.
Failure · zero_infinity=True hides infeasible samples If a target is longer than the encoder output, CTC is impossible — and PyTorch substitutes a zero gradient rather than raising. Pick a bad vocabulary and you silently train on a fraction of your data.
Lesson: read what your loss does with its edge cases.

Theme 3 — measure before you change

Near-miss · "the vocabulary is too big" — it wasn't The plan after the collapse was to blame vocab 1024 and restart at 256. Research showed ESPnet uses 5,000 on the same corpus for 8.4% WER. Had we "fixed" it that way and it happened to work, we'd have learned the wrong lesson permanently. The real cause was warmup: 1,500 vs the reference 15,000.
Lesson: read the reference recipe before changing hyperparameters. One hour of reading beat three days of guessing.
Failure · decode-time attention windowing (TTS) A plausible fix for attention stalls. Measured worse — the model was trained on full-softmax attention and renormalising inside a window hands it context vectors it has never seen.
Lesson: exposure-bias fixes belong in training, not decoding. And: measure your clever idea before keeping it.
Failure · MPS was 13× slower than CPU For the TTS decoder's Python-loop over timesteps, GPU dispatch overhead dominated.
Lesson: accelerators help throughput, not latency of small sequential ops.
Failure · "last" is not "best" — twice The VAD's best hard-case score was 0.806 at step 2750 while the final step scored 0.699. The TTS judge oscillated 0.96 → 0.61 with three regressions.
Lesson: select checkpoints on the metric you care about, and expect edge-case behaviour to oscillate while the loss looks flat.

Theme 4 — the operational ones

Failure · pkill -f matched its own command line The kill command contained the pattern it was searching for, so it killed itself before killing the target. Result: two training runs shared one GPU for 40 minutes, halving throughput (1.53 → 0.42 it/s) and interleaving two writers into one log file, producing a sparse file that made `tail` show stale data.
Lesson: kill by PID or process group. And when two things write one log, you get neither.
Failure · num_workers=0 starved the GPU 42% GPU utilisation — the accelerator idle half the time waiting for log-mels on the main thread.
Lesson: check utilisation before concluding a model is slow. 6 workers took it to 96%.
Failure · optimised for dollars, ignored the real cost Ran a 27-minute reproduction on a laptop to "save" four cents of GPU time, and cooked the machine.
Lesson: cost has more than one unit.

0.6Ten things that transfer to any speech project

  1. Build the eval set before the model. Not a benchmark — the specific things that break. Module 1b's hard cases (zeros, hum, babble, quiet speech) took ten minutes to write and decided which checkpoint shipped.
  2. Log a signal specific to each failure mode. Loss and the headline metric are both blind to blank collapse.
  3. Verify capabilities by exercising them. Not is_available(); run the actual operation.
  4. Artefacts that must agree should fingerprint each other. Tokenizer ↔ checkpoint, manifest ↔ audio on disk.
  5. Read the reference recipe first. Hyperparameter archaeology is cheaper than hyperparameter search.
  6. p50 and p95, decomposed per stage. An aggregate latency number tells you nothing about what to fix.
  7. Augmentation is worth more than data below ~1000 hours — but regularising a model that hasn't learned the task yet only lengthens its plateau.
  8. Streaming claims need a test, and the test needs a counter-test that fails when the property is removed.
  9. Resume must carry the optimizer and the step, not just weights — otherwise the "resume" is a silent quality regression.
  10. An enhancement front-end can hurt a strong downstream model. Measured: denoising raised Whisper's CER at 0 dB. Never assume "cleaner is better" without measuring the consumer.

0.7Questions you can now answer

Explain CTC. Why does it need a blank symbol? You have T audio frames and U output tokens with no alignment between them. CTC defines a many-to-one collapse (merge repeats, drop blanks) and sums the probability of every frame-labelling that collapses to the target, via a forward-backward recursion. The blank does two jobs: it lets the model emit "nothing" at a frame it is unsure about, and it separates genuine repeats — without a blank between them, "hello" collapses to "helo".
CTC vs RNN-T vs attention encoder-decoder — when would you pick each? CTC assumes conditional independence given the audio, so it has no language model and needs an external LM or a second head; it is the cheapest and streams trivially. RNN-T adds a prediction network over output history — an internal LM — while keeping monotonic alignment, which is why it is the production default for streaming. AED (Whisper) has the strongest language modelling and the best accuracy offline, but its attention is not monotonic, so it streams badly and can hallucinate. Hybrid CTC/attention takes CTC's monotonicity as a stabiliser and the decoder as the LM, on one encoder.
How do you make a Conformer stream? Two things, and both are required. Chunked attention masking, so a frame only attends within its chunk plus some history; and a causal depthwise convolution, left-padded only — otherwise the conv module leaks ~15 frames of future and the mask is a fiction. Train with the chunk size resampled every batch and one set of weights serves every latency operating point. Cost on our measurements: 3% relative WER for 640 ms of lookahead.
Why does barge-in dictate the threading model? Barge-in means detecting speech while the agent is talking. VAD is sub-millisecond so it runs inline, but ASR and TTS are hundreds of milliseconds of CPU. If they ran on the event loop, the WebSocket would stop draining microphone audio — so the agent would be deaf exactly while it speaks. They go in worker threads, and an interruption cancels the in-flight reply task and tells the client to flush its playback buffer.
How would you detect end-of-turn? A fixed silence timeout is the baseline everyone ships and it forces an unwinnable trade: 500 ms cuts people off, 900 ms feels laggy. The modern answer is a small audio-native model asking "does this person expect me to reply now?" from prosody and syntax — falling pitch and a completed clause mean go, a held vowel or "my number is four two…" means wait. Smart Turn v3 is ~8M parameters and runs in 12 ms on CPU.
Why might a noise suppressor make ASR worse? Because a strong ASR was trained on noisy audio and has learned to hear through it, while a denoiser hands it spectral artefacts it has never seen — an out-of-distribution input. We measured it: at 0 dB SNR the classical front-end raised Whisper's CER from 0.068 to 0.079, while a model trained on clean data preferred the denoised input. Hence: clean audio to the VAD, original audio to the ASR.
What is CTC blank collapse and how do you detect it? A degenerate local minimum where emitting blank at every frame is a safe, finite-loss answer. The loss goes flat at a plausible value and WER pins at 1.0, so neither reveals it. Detect it by logging the fraction of frames whose CTC argmax is blank: healthy is ~0.85–0.95 and falling, 1.000 is dead. The documented cause is an aggressive early learning rate, and the documented mitigation is a longer warmup.
Why is learning-rate warmup necessary for attention models? At initialisation the attention distribution is near-uniform, so gradients through the softmax are large and badly conditioned. A full-size step there lands the model in a bad basin it does not leave. Reference ASR recipes use 15,000–35,000 warmup steps; we used 1,500 and our CTC head collapsed at step 1,200 — the LR reached peak at 1,500.
How do you evaluate an ASR system properly? WER, not CER, because CER flatters ("recogniton" is 1/10 characters but 1/1 words). Aggregate as total errors ÷ total reference words, never the mean of per-utterance WERs — that weights a 2-word utterance like a 30-word one. Hold out by speaker, not by utterance. Report the operating points separately (greedy / rescored / streaming) because the gaps between them are the information. And report RTF on a named machine.
Build or fine-tune? Fine-tune, almost always. Whisper and Parakeet are free and trained on ~640k hours; you will not beat them at the general task. From-scratch pretraining is justified only by a specific gap — a language or channel they cover badly, licensing, or cost at scale. 500 hours of your own domain audio is worth more than 50,000 hours of audiobooks, and fine-tuning is a $100–500 job against a $2–10k one.

0.8If you build a voice product next — the playbook

stagedo thiswhy this order
0Ship pretrained models behind your own pipeline. Start collecting a replayable eval corpus from real traffic on day one.You cannot improve what you cannot measure, and traffic data compounds.
1Fix turn-taking before touching model quality.Users perceive responsiveness before accuracy. A fixed timeout is the biggest felt defect in most agents.
2Fine-tune ASR on your domain with channel augmentation.Beats any general model on your traffic, for ~1% of pretraining cost.
3Add contextual biasing, ITN, punctuation.Decode-side and post-processing wins that customers notice more than WER.
4Only now consider training something from scratch.Ownership, not accuracy. Be honest about which you are buying.
The one-line version Everyone's models are free and roughly equal. Your advantage is your channel, your domain vocabulary, your turn-taking, and your evaluation loop — none of which come from a model checkpoint.

0.9What this project does not establish

  • That from-scratch models are competitive. They are not, and were never meant to be. The scratch ASR is a teaching artefact.
  • That our numbers are publication-grade. Held-out shares speakers with training; the honest comparison needs real test-clean.
  • That the pipeline is production-hardened. No AEC, no telephony codec path, no autoscaling, single process, no streaming ASR wrapper yet.
  • That any of this works on a laptop microphone. LibriSpeech is read audiobook speech; spontaneous speech through a laptop mic is a domain gap no amount of training on that corpus closes.
