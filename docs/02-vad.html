2.1The problem, precisely

Input: a stream of short audio windows (10–32 ms). Output per window: P(speech). It looks like trivial binary classification. What makes it a real problem is the negative class: silence is easy, but the world is full of speech-shaped non-speech — music, TV in the background, keyboard clatter, babble from the next desk, your own agent's voice. VAD quality is almost entirely about how hard your negatives were at training time.

Why not just an energy threshold?

Energy ("is it loud?") fails both directions: quiet speech (whisper, far mic) is speech, and a slammed door is not. Speech has structure that loudness doesn't capture:

  • Harmonics — voiced sounds are a fundamental (~80–250 Hz) plus stacked overtones, shaped by vocal-tract resonances (formants).
  • Modulation rhythm — energy pulses at the ~4–7 Hz syllable rate; steady noise doesn't.

Your synthetic generator (src/vad/data.py) manufactures exactly these two cues — which is why a tiny model can learn them, and (see 2.5) why it breaks on inputs that violate its synthetic world.

2.2Your VADNet: what its parts are for

log-mel (T, 80) │ treat 80 mel bins as channels, time as the conv axis ▼ Conv1d(80→64, k=5) + BN + ReLU ×3 receptive field: 13 frames ≈ 130 ms ▼ Conv1d(64→1, k=1) per-frame logit ▼ sigmoid → P(speech) per 10 ms frame ~55k params

The conv-over-time is the load-bearing idea: a frame is far more likely to be speech if its neighbours are. Giving each output a 130 ms window bakes the temporal-continuity prior into the architecture instead of bolting it on afterwards. Everything else (BN, ReLU) is plumbing.

Streaming it (src/pipeline/scratch_engines.py)

Training saw whole utterances; live audio arrives in chunks. Two edge problems and their fixes: (1) a chunk's first frames would lose their left-context → keep a rolling buffer of the last 12 mel frames and prepend it; (2) analysis frames are 400 samples but hop is 160 → keep the 240-sample overlap tail between pushes. No look-ahead is used: future context would sharpen boundaries but every frame of look-ahead is a frame of added latency — the eternal streaming trade-off.

2.3Silero VAD: what a production VAD does differently

Your VADNetSilero VAD v5
Params / size~55k / 220 kB~1.8 MB ONNX
Architecture1-D conv stack (feed-forward)learned filterbank encoder + recurrent state carried across windows
Training datasynthetic tones + noise (yours, minutes)~100+ languages, thousands of hours, curated hard negatives (music, babble, clicks)
Interfaceany chunking (we frame internally)exactly 512-sample windows @16 kHz; reset_states() between streams
Cost<1 ms/win CPU<1 ms/win CPU

Read that table bottom-up: the run cost is identical and the architecture is only mildly fancier. The entire quality difference is data. That's the recurring theme of this repo: for small speech models, architecture is rarely the moat; training distribution is. The RNN state also explains a subtlety: Silero accumulates evidence across windows, so each concurrent stream needs its own model instance — which is why the factory builds VADs per session.

Also seen in the wild WebRTC VAD — a 1990s-style GMM on spectral features; ultra-cheap, very trigger-happy on noise; fine as a first gate. Energy gates in telephony — still used as pre-filters. The modern default for self-hosted stacks is Silero; cloud ASR APIs embed their own VAD server-side.

2.4Endpointing: the layer beginners miss

VAD answers a 32 ms question. The orchestrator needs a turn-level answer: "the user has finished talking — ship the utterance to ASR." That's a policy state machine (src/pipeline/endpointing.py), and each knob exists because of a specific failure you'd otherwise ship:

Knob (default)Failure it prevents
start_trigger_ms (96)a cough or click opening a turn (debounce)
pre_roll_ms (320)clipped first words: VAD confirmation is late by construction, so keep a ring buffer of audio from before the trigger — otherwise "seventy" arrives at ASR as "eventy"
end_silence_ms (700)too short → cuts users off mid-thought at every pause; too long → the agent feels laggy. THE latency/accuracy dial (see pipeline page)
hysteresis: end_threshold (0.35) < start_threshold (0.5)mid-word probability dips ending the turn — same two-threshold trick as your Module 1 gate, one level up
min_utterance_ms (250)shipping a 100 ms blip to ASR (Whisper will happily hallucinate a sentence from it)
max_utterance_s (30)unbounded buffering when something (a fan, self-echo) pins VAD high

Because it's pure logic over (window, probability) pairs, it's exhaustively unit-tested (tests/test_endpointing.py) — no models, no audio files, milliseconds. Keeping policy separate from models is what makes that possible.

2.5A real bug: the domain-mismatch lesson

While wiring the e2e test we padded the utterance with digital zeros to simulate the user going quiet. Measured behaviour of your VAD on the padding:

0.335
mean P(speech) on exact zeros — flickers at the threshold
0.004
mean P(speech) on mic-like noise (σ=0.005)

The endpointer never closed the turn. Why: the training generator always added a faint background noise floor, so "perfect digital silence" (log-mel = a uniform −23 wall) is out-of-distribution — the model had literally never seen it. Real microphones always have a noise floor, so the fix was to make the test realistic (pad with quiet noise), not to retrain.

The general lesson (train/serve skew) Models don't fail loudly on out-of-distribution input — they return confident-looking garbage. The inputs your serving path can produce (digital silence, clipped int16, resampler artifacts, wrong gain) must exist in training, or be normalized away before the model. Production speech teams keep "input hygiene" test suites for exactly this.

...and the same bug, live, in the other direction

First real-microphone session with the scratch VAD: P(speech) pinned high on room ambience — a real browser mic (with its noise-suppression processing, room tone, and gain) is as out-of-distribution for the synthetic-data model as digital zeros were, just in the opposite direction. The failure cascaded beautifully: turns never ended (both closed at the 30 s hard cap — visible in /metrics as utterance_ms p50 = 30000), and the moment the agent started answering, the pinned VAD triggered a phantom barge-in that flushed the reply — "it's not responding" was actually "it responded and immediately interrupted itself".

Two fixes came out of it: the client now shows a live P(speech) readout (the single most useful debug signal in the pipeline — near 0 in silence and near 1 while you talk means healthy), and the practical remedy is VOICE_VAD_ENGINE=silero, whose thousands of hours of real-mic training data are precisely the moat described in 2.3. Making the scratch VAD mic-robust would mean training on real speech + real noise (LibriSpeech + MUSAN) instead of synthesis — a great exercise.

2.6Module 1b: the real-audio retrain (the fix, from scratch)

The repair for 2.5 keeps the 55k-param architecture byte-identical and replaces only the training data (src/vad/data_real.py, trained by scripts/09_train_vad_real.py) — deliberately proving that for small speech models, the data is the model. The recipe is a miniature of how real VAD training sets (MUSAN-style) are built:

  • Real speech, free labels. Speech clips come from LibriSpeech (real voices, all ~2,700 dev-clean utterances). Utterances contain pauses, so labeling every frame "speech" would re-teach the 2.5 bug — instead, per-frame energy is gated on the clean clip (within 30 dB of its peak = speech), and only then is noise mixed on top. The labels stay ground-truth because they were made before the corruption.
  • Hard negatives = what a mic actually sends. Colored noise from faint to loud (room tone), babble built by summing 6 real overlapping speakers (MUSAN's own trick — the hardest negative, speech-band energy without single-voice structure), mains hum, near-silence, and pure digital zeros — the 2.5 out-of-distribution input, now in-domain by construction.
  • Augmentation = the serving path's dirt. Random SNR (0–25 dB), random gain (−20–0 dB), occasional clipping; half the examples contain no speech at all, because a VAD must be confident about pure background, not merely prefer speech.

The training script ends with the eval that matters: mean P(speech) on exactly the scenarios that failed live. Checkpoints are selected on that score, not taken from the last step — measured trajectory 0.67 → 0.81 with repeated regressions (the final step scored 0.70, and one intermediate run pinned digital zeros at 0.91!); same oscillation lesson as the TTS module. The shipped checkpoint:

0.009
digital zeros (was the OOD bug)
0.01–0.04
room noise / hum (was ≈1 live)
0.78
speech, clean or over noise
0.87
held-out frame accuracy

Honest soft spots: 6-speaker babble sits at ≈0.50 (right at the threshold — genuinely the hardest negative) and very quiet speech (−26 dB) at ≈0.47 may fail to trigger. The pipeline picks the new checkpoint up automatically (scratch_vad_ckpt="auto" prefers vad_real.pt).

2.7Evaluation: how VAD quality is actually measured

  • Frame level: precision/recall per frame (your Module 1 accuracy is the toy version). Almost never what users feel.
  • Boundary level: how late is speech onset detected (clipping risk), how late is the endpoint (latency)? Reported as onset/offset latency distributions.
  • System level: did ASR WER change? did turnaround p95 change? A VAD is only ever as good as the pipeline it gates — the reason we expose /metrics rather than a standalone VAD score.
